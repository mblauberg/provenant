# Argument structure and paragraphing

Load with `legal-concision-and-anti-ai.md` for submissions and written
advocacy. Each paragraph makes one point, nothing is re-argued, and a judicial
reader gets question, answer and reasons at a glance. No rule here strips an
honest legal negative or source boundary.

## Write for the decision-maker

- Draft submissions capable, if the tribunal agrees, of forming the substance of
  the reasons. Written submissions are the first half of the appeal.
- Assume a busy generalist meeting the matter cold. Orient; never assume
  familiarity.
- Get to the operative statute, contract or order text fast, inside the point it
  governs. Long background before it reads as avoidance.
- Use a dated, sourced chronology where the forum allows, to keep narrative out
  of the argument.

## Front-load

- A fresh reader can state the relief, grounds and why within about 90 seconds.
- State the deep issue concretely, embedding the decisive fact (`whether the
  discretion miscarried where both eyewitnesses had died`), never `whether the
  tribunal erred`. Legal premise, factual premise, then question, in about 75
  words.
- Make any summary of argument detachable and complete, issue by issue.
- Whole document: upshot paragraph (relief, grounds, why), deep issue, then a
  detachable summary where length warrants. Single ground: open with the
  one-sentence ground-answer carrying the decisive fact. Do not stack devices.
- Test the document cold: recover relief, grounds and reasons from the headings
  and each paragraph's first sentence alone. Where not, fix the structure
  before polishing.

## Headings and paragraphs

- Argument-bearing headings assert the section's conclusion (`The reasons
  disclose no finding on the agreed date`), not a topic label. Extracted alone,
  they state the case. Procedural-history and umbrella sections may stay labels.
- Open each paragraph with a contestable contention, develop one point, close on
  its conclusion in a sentence or two (CRAC/CREAC). If the caption needs `X and
  separately Y`, split. In a rule paragraph, state the broad principle first,
  then qualifications; order authorities highest court to lowest.
- Synthesise fact clusters: when several facts make the same small point, state
  the pattern and its conclusion once as the topic sentence, then hang instances
  beneath as an anchor list with their own pinpoints. Synthesise within one
  issue; never fuse distinct legal questions into one `errors` block.

## State it once

- State each shared premise, rule, figure and chronology once, in the framework
  block; later sections cite it by name. Restatement survives only at the
  opening frame and one close, carrying new evidence or stakes. A repetitious
  filing is skimmed, so cutting repetition is persuasion, not housekeeping.
- Ration protective formulas: full at the defined home, the first grounds-body
  use and the per-ground close; a bare node cite elsewhere. Never leave a
  deployment site without at least the cite, and keep the full formula at the one
  site that deploys the sensitive material.
- For a filing pack, one point has one home; cross-references name the target
  document and a stable locus (heading, paragraph or schedule number, not a
  page); the same figure, term, date or label reads identically in every
  document.

## Select and aim

- Run the strongest grounds, not every available one; many marginal grounds
  raise a presumption that none has merit. Three or four is an editing
  heuristic, not a limit **(skill default)**; fold overlapping complaints into
  their stronger owner, and do not merge distinct grounds to hit the number.
- Tie every submission to a ground or issue. If it cannot be stated cleanly with
  record references, the defect is in the ground: amend or drop it.
- One argument is one argument. Do not dress it as primary and alternative
  limbs; reserve express alternatives for distinct legal routes. Do not
  double-number (`E.2` and `Limb 2`); use plain ordinals under numbered headings.
- Lead with the strongest ground, threshold first, then strongest to weakest.
  A potentially dispositive jurisdiction ground leads.
- Cut a weak point outright; relocate strong cited analysis to a schedule with a
  pinpoint rather than cutting it. Sentence trimming floors at a few pages; real
  savings come from selection, one-home rule and relocation.
- A schedule is a reference device. Do not smuggle a contention into it, and
  consolidate shared material into one schedule that submissions cite.
- Tables suit short-cell data, figure-to-source maps and compact matrices. They
  rarely shorten reasoned prose. Measure the render before claiming a saving.
- A page limit is a ceiling. When near it, re-run grounds selection and re-home
  analysis; length erodes persuasion before it breaches a rule.
- Hold one organising theme honestly: never bend or soften found facts to
  preserve it.

## Adverse authority and candour

- Verify and meet the current professional-conduct, candour and forum duties on
  adverse authority; where disclosure is required it is not tactical.
- Distinguish on the facts first. Attack an adverse decision's correctness only
  last, and never attack an authority's worth or the opponent's motives.
- Confront the obvious weakness up front, in your own words. Concede a
  non-essential point cleanly; a located concession buys credibility.
- Anticipate, never attribute. Frame an expected answer as expected (`the other
  party may answer that ...`), source it to the record where actually said, and
  never put it in the opponent's mouth (see `source-boundary-and-citations.md`).
- Identify an impugned passage precisely with a pinpoint and meet it as meant.
- Explain, do not label: `hopeless` and `misconceived` do no work; state the
  premise the argument needs and the source that removes it.

## Reply

- Answer the opponent's distinct points in order, with acceptance or rejection
  and a short reasoned pinpoint; cross-refer to the case-in-chief by paragraph.
- Answer the argument at its strongest. Account for every distinct contention:
  answered, adopted, or expressly not pressed and why. Silence reads as
  concession.
- A point with no trace in the written submissions risks `an unattractive air of
  afterthought`.
- Hold predicted-only answers for the authorised reply round. Fold actual
  answers into the deciding ground, not a standalone rebuttal Part.

## Sentence economy

Average about 20 words; over about 24 or with three or more list items, split or
tabulate. Three or more parallel quantifications, each with its own source and
outcome, are a table: preserve every figure, quote and anchor. Vary length; land
the decisive point in the shortest sentence. Prefer active verbs; cut `herein`,
`said`, `pursuant to`, `inter alia`. Cite sparingly: one pinpoint for an
uncontested point; do not string cases for a settled proposition.
