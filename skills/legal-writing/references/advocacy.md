# Submissions and tribunals

## Writing for the decision-maker

Draft submissions that could form the substance of the reasons. Assume a busy
generalist meeting the matter cold: orient, and reach the operative text fast.
Keep narrative in a dated, sourced chronology where the forum allows.

- A fresh reader can state relief, grounds and why in about 90 seconds. State
  the deep issue concretely with the decisive fact embedded ('whether the
  discretion miscarried where both eyewitnesses had died'), not 'whether the
  tribunal erred'. Do not stack devices: a whole document gets an upshot paragraph
  and a detachable summary where length warrants; a single ground opens with a
  one-sentence answer.
- Test the document cold: relief, grounds and reasons recoverable from the
  headings and first sentences alone. If not, fix structure before polishing.
- Argument headings state the section's conclusion, not a topic. Open each
  paragraph with a contestable contention, develop one point, close on its
  conclusion. In a rule paragraph, state the principle first, then
  qualifications, authorities highest court first.
- Synthesise fact clusters into a pattern stated once, with instances beneath as
  an anchor list. Never fuse distinct legal questions into one block of 'errors'.

## State it once

Each shared premise, rule, figure and chronology appears once, in a framework
block, and later sections cite it by name. Restatement is for the opening frame
and one close, carrying new evidence or stakes. In a filing pack, one point has
one home; cross-references name the target document and a stable locus (heading,
paragraph or schedule number, not a page); a figure, term, date or label reads
identically everywhere. Cutting repetition is persuasion, not housekeeping.

## Select and aim

- Run the strongest grounds. Three or four is an editing heuristic **(default)**,
  not a limit; fold overlapping complaints into their stronger owner and do not
  merge distinct grounds to hit a number.
- Tie every submission to a ground or issue. Lead with a potentially dispositive
  jurisdiction ground, then strongest to weakest.
- One argument is one argument: no primary and alternative limbs unless they are
  distinct legal routes, and no double numbering.
- Cut a weak point outright. Relocate strong cited analysis to a schedule with a
  pinpoint. Savings come from selection, one home and relocation, not sentence
  trimming. A schedule is a reference device and carries no new contention.
- Tables suit short-cell data and figure-to-source maps, not reasoned prose.
  Measure the render before claiming a saving. A page limit is a ceiling: near
  it, re-select grounds and re-home analysis.

## Candour and reply

- Meet current professional-conduct and forum duties on adverse authority;
  where disclosure is required it is not tactical. Distinguish on the facts
  first; attack correctness last; never attack an authority's worth or the
  opponent's motives.
- Confront the obvious weakness up front and concede a non-essential point with
  a located concession. Explain instead of labelling: 'hopeless' and
  'misconceived' do no work.
- Anticipate, never attribute: frame an expected answer as expected and source
  it only where actually said.
- Reply answers each distinct point in order with a pinpointed reason, at its
  strongest. Account for every contention: answered, adopted, or expressly not
  pressed. Silence reads as concession. A point absent from the written
  submissions risks 'an unattractive air of afterthought'. Hold predicted-only
  answers for the authorised reply round.

## Tribunals and merits review

A tribunal's name does not fix its procedure. Record the division or list, the
enabling and substantive Acts, whether the matter is original, internal review,
external review or appeal, the time limit and any precondition, and the practice
notes and costs, representation and evidence rules for that list. Verify
against official pages; this skill supplies no jurisdiction map or time limit.

What changes from a court:

- Evidence rules are relaxed but the tribunal must act fairly on logically
  probative material, so anchor every fact anyway.
- Register is plainer. Write for a member who wants the criteria addressed, not
  a set-piece address. Leave is often needed for representation and many parties
  are self-represented, so do not draft tactically against them.
- Do not import court costs assumptions or threats; many enabling Acts displace
  the default.
- Ask only for a disposition the tribunal can make (affirm, vary, set aside,
  substitute, remit). If it cannot give the outcome, say so and name the forum
  that can.

Merits review asks what the correct or preferable decision is on the material,
not whether the decision-maker erred.

- Address each statutory criterion, using the criteria as headings where the list
  permits. Identify the date at which facts are assessed and whether later
  material may be considered; schemes differ.
- Separate findings of fact, discretion and jurisdictional preconditions and argue
  each in its own terms. Do not argue judicial review grounds in a merits review
  or the reverse; if a genuine legal error matters, name it specifically and
  obtain qualified review of the route.
- Keep facts in the facts document and contentions in the contentions or
  submissions; blending them invites the other side to dispute the whole.
- Lead with the outcome sought and the criteria in issue. Pinpoint into the
  bundle by the tribunal's own pagination. Answer the respondent's stated reasons
  rather than restating history. Concede early what should be conceded.
- Appeal routes from tribunals are narrow, body-specific and short-limited:
  verify route, time limit and court from official sources and obtain qualified
  review before advising.
